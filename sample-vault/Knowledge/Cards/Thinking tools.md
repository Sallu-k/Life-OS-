Sample cards: ways to think more clearly.

## Base rates
Before judging a case by its details, ask how common it is in general. A positive result from a 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 disease is still most likely a false alarm.
Source: Wikipedia, Base rate fallacy
Q: What do people ignore in the base rate fallacy?
A: How common the thing is in general

## Fermi estimates
Enrico Fermi estimated hard numbers by breaking them into rough parts you can guess (how many piano tuners in Chicago?). Errors in the parts tend to cancel out.
Source: Wikipedia, Fermi problem

## Occam's razor
Among explanations that fit the facts equally well, prefer the one with fewest assumptions.
Source: Wikipedia, Occam's razor
Q: Occam's razor prefers the explanation with…?
A: The fewest assumptions

## Correlation is not causation
Two things rising together may share a hidden cause or be coincidence: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer.
Source: Wikipedia, Correlation does not imply causation

## Survivorship bias
In WWII, Abraham Wald advised armouring the parts of returning planes that had no bullet holes: planes hit there never came back.
Source: Wikipedia, Survivorship bias
Q: Why armour the places on returning planes with no holes?
A: Planes hit there did not return (survivorship bias)

## Confirmation bias
We notice and remember evidence that supports what we already believe; actively looking for evidence that would prove you wrong is the remedy.
Source: Wikipedia, Confirmation bias

## Second-order thinking
Ask "and then what?": the first effect of a decision is often not the one that matters most.
Source: Howard Marks, The Most Important Thing

## Inversion
Charlie Munger's advice, after the mathematician Jacobi: "Invert, always invert." To solve a problem, also ask what would guarantee failure, and avoid that.
Source: Wikipedia, Charlie Munger
Q: What does "invert" mean as a thinking tool?
A: Ask what would cause failure, and avoid it

## Sunk cost fallacy
Money or time already spent cannot be recovered, so it should not decide what you do next; judge only future costs and benefits.
Source: Wikipedia, Sunk cost
Q: Should past spending decide future choices?
A: No: only future costs and benefits matter

## Regression to the mean
After an unusually good or bad result, the next one tends to be closer to average by chance alone, so praise or blame may look like it "worked".
Source: Wikipedia, Regression toward the mean

## The map is not the territory
A model is a simplification of reality, useful because it leaves things out; problems start when we forget what it leaves out.
Source: Wikipedia, Map–territory relation

## Opportunity cost
The real cost of anything is what you give up to get it, including the best alternative use of your time.
Source: Wikipedia, Opportunity cost
Q: What is opportunity cost?
A: The value of the best alternative you give up

## Dunning–Kruger effect
In Dunning and Kruger's studies, people with the least skill in an area tended to overestimate their ability most.
Source: Wikipedia, Dunning–Kruger effect

## Steelmanning
Before disagreeing, state the other side's argument in its strongest form; if you can't, you don't understand it yet.
Source: Wikipedia, Straw man

## Pareto principle
In many systems, roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes; find the few causes that matter most.
Source: Wikipedia, Pareto principle
Q: What does the Pareto principle say?
A: Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes

## Feedback loops
A process whose output feeds back into it either grows (reinforcing loop, like compound interest) or settles (balancing loop, like a thermostat).
Source: Wikipedia, Feedback

## Compound interest
Small regular gains multiply: improving 1% a week makes you about 1.68 times better in a year.
Source: Wikipedia, Compound interest
Q: Improving 1% a week for a year multiplies by about…?
A: 1.68

## Hanlon's razor
Do not assume bad intent where carelessness or misunderstanding explains it.
Source: Wikipedia, Hanlon's razor

## Goodhart's law
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure": people optimise the number instead of what it stood for.
Source: Wikipedia, Goodhart's law
Q: What happens when a measure becomes a target (Goodhart)?
A: It stops being a good measure

## Chesterton's fence
Don't remove a fence until you know why it was put there; old rules often solved problems you can't see.
Source: Wikipedia, G. K. Chesterton
